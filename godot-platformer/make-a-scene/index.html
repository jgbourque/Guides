Godot Modding · Tutorial 02

What is a scene? Build one and find out.

A scene in Godot is a reusable game component — something you build once and use as many times as you want. It can be huge, like a whole level. Or tiny, like a single coin. Scenes are made out of nodes (Godot's built-in components) and sometimes other, smaller scenes you've already built. The fastest way to understand them is to build one. Let's do that.

Step 1 of 4

What's inside a scene?

Every scene is a tree of nodes. Godot has hundreds of node types, and each one already knows how to do its job. A Sprite2D displays an image. An AudioStreamPlayer plays a sound. No code needed — though you can attach a script later for custom behavior.

To build one, you pick the nodes you need, arrange them in a tree, and save the file. A scene can also contain other scenes you've made — that's how bigger ones (like whole levels) get put together: nodes plus smaller scenes as building blocks.

Once you save it, you can use it over and over without rebuilding. Need ten platforms? Drag your platform scene into the level ten times. Each one is an instance of the same saved scene.

Change the saved scene later — like swapping a coin's collision shape — and every copy you've already placed updates too.

Remember

A scene can be as small as a single platform, or as big as a whole level.

Every node lives inside one other node — its parent. A parent can hold any number of nodes inside it — those are its children. The node at the top of the whole tree, the one that holds everything else, is called the root. (The root doesn't have a parent — it's at the top.)

This isn't just a way of organizing things. It changes how the nodes behave together.

Parent moves, children follow. Children inherit a lot from their parent. Move the parent, and the children move with it. Rotate or scale the parent, the children do the same. Hide the parent, the children disappear too. That's why putting the heart sprite inside the platform (as a child of it) means the heart automatically rides along when you move the platform around. You don't have to move them separately. The same rule applies when one scene is nested inside another — the whole nested scene moves as one piece along with its parent.

That's it. Three nodes put together, one shape attached, one file saved. A whole platform.

Why those three nodes?

Many of the basic interactive objects you'd build in a 2D platformer at least start with these three kinds of nodes: a body (handles physics — like getting hit by other things, or holding still while things land on you), a collision shape (tells the body what its shape is for hits), and a sprite (the picture you see). The body holds the shape and the sprite as its children, so they all move together. Coins, doors, enemies, your player — they often start from this same three-piece base, with small differences for what each one does.

Now jump into Godot and try it yourself. Same three nodes, same flow — your own scene by the end.

Step 3 of 4

Drop it into a level.

You've saved OurPlatform.tscn. Now we'll put it to use. Once you know the moves, you can drop as many copies as you want anywhere in your game.

Open main.tscn (the big level scene). There are two ways to drop your saved scene into a level, and one more move for when you need to come back and edit the saved scene itself.

1
Drag it into the viewport

This is the most common move. Drag OurPlatform.tscn up out of the FileSystem (bottom-left) and into the viewport (center) of main.tscn. A copy gets placed wherever you drop it. Do this five times, you have five platforms.

While you drag, Godot shows a little tooltip that tells you how holding Shift or Alt changes where the new copy lands in your scene tree:

Dropping a Scene file…
DefaultAdd as sibling of selected node (except when root is selected)
Hold ShiftAdd as child of selected node
Hold AltAdd as child of root node

Handy for keeping things organized — like dropping coins as children of a Coins group node by holding Shift while Coins is selected.

2
Drag it into the Scene tree

Drag the file straight up from the FileSystem into the Scene tree instead of the viewport. A blue line shows you exactly where it'll land in the tree. Useful when you want it to sit in a specific spot in the tree and don't care yet about where it shows up on screen.

3
Double-click it to make changes later

If you need to come back and adjust OurPlatform.tscn itself — change its color, swap its shape, whatever — double-click it in the FileSystem to open it in its own tab. Changes you save there show up in every copy you've placed (except for the ones you've customized specifically).

The big idea

Every copy you drop into main.tscn is linked back to the original. Edit OurPlatform.tscn — change the heart to a star, swap the circle for a rectangle — and every copy picks up the change.

There are some exceptions: anything you've already tweaked directly on one specific copy in the level (like its position, scale, or modulate color) stays the way you left it. For everything you haven't touched, though, the source change wins.

Try it: open OurPlatform.tscn, change the Sprite2D's texture to a different image, save, then open main.tscn. Every platform that you haven't customized will be showing the new image. One edit, whole level updated.

Step 4 of 4

What's next.

You've built a scene, you've placed it in a level. That's the core loop of working in Godot — almost everything you'll ever build is some version of those moves. Here are three ways to practice.

01

Build something new with a custom shape.

Build a fresh scene the same way you just did — Node2D root, StaticBody2D as its child, CollisionShape2D under that, Sprite2D as sibling. This time use your own image for the texture, and pick a shape that fits it. RectangleShape2D works for blocky art, but if your image has an irregular outline, try ConvexPolygonShape2D and set its points so the collision matches the shape of the picture. Why this matters: the three-node pattern is flexible. Same recipe, different parts, different result.

02

Place ten OurPlatform copies in main.tscn.

Drag, drag, drag. Notice how identical they all are — they're not separate copies you'd have to update one-by-one, they all point back to the same source scene. Now open OurPlatform.tscn and change something. Every copy you haven't customized picks the change up. Why this matters: this is the difference between copy-pasting things and actually reusing them.

03

Customize one copy on purpose.

Click one of your platforms in main.tscn and change the Sprite2D's Texture in the Inspector. Just for that one. Notice how the property name now has a small revert arrow next to it — Godot is telling you "this one's been customized, I won't change it." Why this matters: every copy picks up changes from the source scene — except for the parts you've changed on a specific copy.

You now know what a scene is, how to build one, and how to drop it into a level so it can be reused. Scenes are the building block of most of what you'll work with in Godot — UI panels are scenes, enemy AI is scenes, your player is a scene, the title screen is a scene. It's all trees of nodes saved as files.

From here, you can start building.
